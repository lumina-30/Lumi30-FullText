                                                 Abstract
          Advanced-AI safety spans alignment, capability evaluation, control, monitoring, human
     oversight, governance, and systemic-risk analysis. Existing work already treats irreversibility,
     distributed trust, meaningful human control, runtime assurance, gradual human disempowerment,
     viability, and safety cases. The contribution attempted here is therefore not priority over those
     ideas, but a stronger operational composition for claims about whether a socio-technical system
     remains human-revisable before irreversible transitions.
          The Pre-Irreversibility Safety Framework (PISF) is organized around three analytically
     separable candidate claims. Distributed Irreversible Authority asks whether any tolerated
     coalition of human or artificial authority domains, after accounting for common dependencies
     and the currently reachable state envelope, can force a transition into an irreversible region.
     Effective Pre-Irreversibility Refusal asks whether a causally effective refusal can take effect
     before a conservative last-safe-intervention bound; it distinguishes the latency of restraint from
     the latency of verifying that restraint. Human-Revisable Development Viability asks
     whether human-governed policies can robustly keep development inside a declared revisability
     region while preserving more than a single path-locked future.
          The framework associates these claims with: (i) a family of minimal irreversible authority cut
     sets, with cardinality and compromise-cost summaries; (ii) a robust causal refusal margin; and
     (iii) a finite-horizon human-revisable viability kernel augmented by an optionality measure over
     materially distinct reachable futures. Boundary Completeness, Model Adequacy, and Independent
     Assurance are treated separately as conditions governing whether evidence for the three claims
     is credible. Evidence is classified as Verified, Unresolved, or Failed; unresolved evidence is
     not treated as a pass.
          The paper does not claim that Authority, Refusal, and Revisability form a unique or mathe-
     matically minimal decomposition, nor that satisfying them is sufficient for safe superintelligence.
     Counterexamples support only their diagnostic separability under the declared definitions.
     Worked toy cases show how the revised objects can be recomputed, including a cut-set example,
     a refusal-timing example that separates causal effect from verification, and a viability/optionality
     example. They do not constitute empirical validation of frontier-AI deployments.

cut sets; timely refusal; runtime assurance; human-revisable development; viability; future optionality;
meaningful human control; independent assurance; AI control

1     Introduction
AI safety increasingly operates across several layers. Alignment research asks whether systems
pursue intended objectives. AI control asks whether harmful outcomes can still be prevented when
an untrusted model may actively attempt subversion. Frontier safety frameworks connect capability
thresholds to safeguards and deployment governance. Meaningful-human-control research asks when
human oversight is substantive rather than nominal. Safety-case research asks whether high-level
safety claims can be supported by explicit arguments and evidence. System-safety and viability
research address control, failure propagation, safe sets, and recoverability in complex systems
[13, 24, 10, 14, 16, 6].
    Irreversibility is not a new safety concern. Small-coalition vulnerability, unilateral action,
distributed trust, runtime safety enforcement, and gradual disempowerment each have substantial
prior literatures [8, 9, 20, 17, 15]. The narrower question addressed here is:

      Can claims about pre-irreversibility human control be decomposed into
      operational objects that third parties can inspect, recompute, challenge, and
      revise without assuming that any one actor, model, or evaluator remains
      permanently correct?

    Three recurrent failure structures motivate the decomposition. First, a small coalition may hold
enough effective authority to force an irreversible transition even when monitoring and nominal
oversight exist. Second, refusal may exist formally yet take effect too late. Third, no actor may
deliberately cross a catastrophic boundary, yet ordinary development can progressively remove
fallback capacity, institutional competence, infrastructure diversity, or materially distinct future
choices.
    PISF therefore uses the diagnostic decomposition
                                      PISF claim = A ∧ B ∧ C,                                       (1)
where A is Distributed Irreversible Authority, B is Effective Pre-Irreversibility Refusal, and C is
Human-Revisable Development Viability. This notation is a safety-case organization, not a theorem
that A ∧ B ∧ C is sufficient for safe advanced AI, and not a claim that these are the unique or
minimal safety dimensions.
    Version 1.2 makes five substantive changes to the earlier formulation. First, Core A is re-expressed
using state-conditioned minimal irreversible cut sets rather than treating domain count alone as the
central object. Second, Core B separates causal restraint latency from assurance latency. Third,
Core C separates robust viability from preservation of materially distinct future options. Fourth,
evidence for all three cores is placed behind a stronger independent-assurance condition informed by
roots-of-trust and attestation architectures. Fifth, the paper explicitly treats A/B/C as a revisable
diagnostic decomposition rather than a uniquely established ontology of pre-irreversibility safety.


2     Related Work
2.1   AI control and adversarial safety
Greenblatt et al. define AI control as a second layer of defense in which protocols aim to prevent
harmful outcomes even if an untrusted model attempts intentional subversion [13]. This is directly
relevant to both Core A and Core B: authority structures must not be bypassable by the system
being constrained, and refusal paths should remain effective under the threat model rather than
only during benign operation.

                                                   2
2.2   Meaningful human control and oversight
Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven argue that meaningful human control cannot be reduced to
a human merely being “in the loop” and develop tracking and tracing conditions linking system
behavior to relevant human reasons and responsibility [24]. More recent work on medical AI
similarly argues that oversight is meaningful only when users have epistemic capacity, cognitive
space, decisional authority, and practical intervention effectiveness [25]. These works motivate
treating human authority, information, time, and intervention effectiveness as operational rather
than nominal properties.

2.3   Distributed trust, cut sets, and threshold authorization
Threshold cryptography distributes security-critical operations across multiple parties so that
compromise of a bounded subset need not compromise the protected operation [20]. Fault-tree
analysis uses minimal cut sets to represent minimal combinations of component failures sufficient
to cause a top event [19]. PISF borrows the structural idea of minimal cut sets but applies it to
socio-technical authority: a cut set is a minimal coalition whose effective authority can force an
irreversible transition under a declared threat realization. The analogy is useful but not exact; PISF
authority domains can include people, organizations, credentials, infrastructure, and institutional
procedures.

2.4   Reachability, barrier functions, and viability
Hamilton–Jacobi reachability provides formal tools for analyzing whether nonlinear or hybrid
systems can reach or avoid unsafe sets under control and disturbance [11]. Control barrier functions
characterize forward invariance of safe sets and support real-time safety constraints [1]. Viability
theory studies states from which some admissible policy can keep a system inside a constraint set [6].
PISF does not claim novelty over these control-theoretic objects. It uses their structure to sharpen
questions about human-governed intervention and future revisability in socio-technical AI systems.

2.5   Runtime assurance
The Simplex family of runtime-assurance architectures separates an advanced controller from
a trusted or verified reversionary mechanism and transfers control when safety conditions are
threatened. Black-Box Simplex provides a peer-reviewed formalization in which runtime checks
support safety without requiring the advanced controller itself to be verified [17]. NASA has also
described runtime assurance as a route for integrating AI/ML or otherwise unverified software into
safety-critical systems [18]. PISF treats runtime assurance as one important implementation class
for Core B, not as a complete substitute for civilization-scale authority and revisability analysis.

2.6   Gradual disempowerment and systemic risk
Kulveit et al. analyze gradual disempowerment in which incremental AI adoption can weaken human
influence across economic, cultural, and state systems without requiring an abrupt takeover [15].
This is a strong conceptual predecessor to Core C. PISF therefore does not treat gradual erosion of
human influence as a novel discovery; it asks whether such trajectories can be represented as loss of
robust human-revisability and loss of future optionality.




                                                  3
2.7    Safety cases and independent assurance
Frontier-AI safety-case work proposes explicit claims, arguments, assumptions, and evidence as a
scalable basis for safety review [10, 14]. NIST SP 800-193 distinguishes roots of trust and chains
of trust used to protect, detect, and recover platform state [21]. RFC 9334 separates an Attester,
Evidence, a Verifier, an appraisal policy, and a Relying Party [23]. PISF uses these as mature
assurance precedents: evidence about A/B/C should not be considered independently credible if the
evaluated authority can freely modify the evidence source, appraisal rule, verifier, and acceptance
decision while preserving a nominal pass.

2.8    Frontier-AI risk frameworks
Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework, OpenAI’s
Preparedness Framework, and the International AI Safety Report provide evolving capability,
safeguard, and risk-management structures [2, 12, 22, 7]. PISF is not a competing risk taxonomy.
Evidence produced by such frameworks can feed the narrower A/B/C claims.


3     Problem Formulation
3.1    Analysis specification
Every PISF claim is conditional on a declared analysis specification

                                            Σ = (B, Θ, R, H),                                           (2)

where B is the modeled socio-technical boundary, Θ the threat/failure model, R the human-
revisability specification, and H the evaluation horizon. Application-specific parameters may also
include an authority-tolerance class TA , an intervention safety buffer ∆safe > 0, and optionality
parameters (m, δ) defined below.
    Let xt ∈ X denote the socio-technical state. The modeled boundary may include AI systems and
agents, humans and organizations, credentials, compute, network and cloud dependencies, physical
actuators, monitors, logs, safety mechanisms, and institutional rules. Abstract dynamics may be
written as
                                      xt+1 = F (xt , uH    S
                                                      t , ut , dt ),                           (3)
where uH                                                                          S
        t denotes actions permitted by the human-governed control structure, ut other system or
actor actions, and dt disturbances or environmental changes. The symbols do not imply that these

3.2    Revisability and irreversibility
Let VR ⊆ X denote states satisfying the declared revisability specification. Such a specification may
include continued human-governed authority over consequential changes, independent information
channels, fallback capability, ability to replace or disable critical AI services, infrastructure diversity,
and preservation of materially distinct feasible alternatives.
    A temporary exit from VR need not be irreversible. Define IR ⊆ X as the states from which no
feasible human-governed policy can restore the declared revisability specification within the stated
time, resource, authority, and physical constraints. Both VR and IR are specification-relative and
must not be silently treated as universal moral truths.

4     Core A: Distributed Irreversible Authority
4.1   Authority domains and dependency closure
Let the primitive authority domains be

                                           A = {a1 , . . . , an }.                                    (4)

An authority domain is a set of permissions, credentials, infrastructure, procedures, or decision rights
treated as jointly compromisable under Θ. Numerical plurality is not independence: a thousand
model instances that depend on one root administrator, identity provider, or release credential may
still form a single effective authority domain.
     For threat realization θ ∈ Θ and coalition K ⊆ A, let Clθ (K) denote the dependency closure
induced by compromise or capture of K.

4.2   Reachable-envelope forcing predicate
The previous formulation counted a coalition as irreversible-forcing if it could force irreversibility
from some relevant state. Version 1.2 binds this claim to the evaluated state x0 and horizon H.
    Let Reachθ (x0 , H) denote the states reachable from x0 within the declared horizon under the
modeled admissible evolution for threat realization θ, before crediting coalition K with the irreversible
forcing step whose existence is being tested. The envelope may include ordinary operation, modeled
disturbances, and strategic behavior already admitted by Θ; it must not be inflated by assuming
the very irreversible transition that IFθ is meant to diagnose. Define

                                          IFθ (K | x0 , H) = 1                                        (5)

iff there exists x ∈ Reachθ (x0 , H) such that authority available through Clθ (K) is sufficient to
force a trajectory into IR without the active cooperation of an uncompromised authority domain.
Otherwise IFθ (K | x0 , H) = 0.
     The word “force” is threat-model relative. If Θ includes strategic resistance by uncompromised
domains, IFθ = 1 requires the coalition to succeed despite that modeled resistance; if such resistance
has not been modeled, the stronger forcing claim remains unresolved rather than silently assumed.
This state-conditioning prevents a remote or unreachable hypothetical state from automatically
lowering the current authority threshold.

5     Core B: Effective Pre-Irreversibility Refusal
5.1   Executable refusal
For state x and hazard h, let UR (x, h) denote practically executable refusal actions, including
pause, STOP, isolation, rollback, credential revocation, resource denial, degraded safe operation, or
redirection. A nominal right or user-interface control is insufficient unless it can causally alter the
relevant trajectory.

5.2   Causal effect latency and assurance latency
Version 1.1 included verification time in the same latency scalar as detection, decision, authorization,
and enforcement. That conflated two questions: when restraint takes effect, and when an evaluator
can confirm that it took effect.
    At evaluation or trigger time t0 , define τeffect (h) as the remaining latency until effective causal
restraint of hazard h. If the relevant trigger has not yet been detected, the bound must include the
remaining detection delay; after a pre-authorized trigger has fired, already-completed steps are not
counted again. Its critical path may include detection, decision, authorization, and enforcement,
while pre-authorized machine-speed policies can move some steps outside the runtime critical path.
Define τassure (h) as the remaining latency from t0 to obtain evidence that the restraint has taken
effect and remains within the declared boundary.
    In general,
                                         τeffect (h) ≤ τassure (h),                                  (10)
but neither equality nor strict inequality is universal.

Core B does not require a human to react manually on machine timescales. Humans may pre-
authorize a rule
                                         q ⇒ πR                                     (14)
that triggers refusal policy πR automatically, provided that: (i) the boundary and rule remain subject

thereby acquire unilateral irreversible-GO authority. Runtime-assurance and Simplex architectures
are important implementation classes for such structures [17, 18].


6     Core C: Human-Revisable Development Viability
6.1   Robust viability
Core C addresses a different failure path: every local action may appear permissible and acute
hazards may remain stoppable, yet ordinary development can progressively remove human ability
to pursue materially different futures.
    Let NΘ denote the modeled set of admissible non-human, adversarial, institutional, market,
and environmental trajectories under the declared threat model. Define the finite-horizon robust
human-revisable viability kernel
                        H
                       KR = {x0 ∈ VR : ∃πH ∀ν ∈ NΘ , xt ∈ VR ∀t ∈ [0, H]} ,                        (15)

where πH is a policy permitted by the human-governed institutional structure. Automated controls
are allowed if the relevant constitutional authority remains human-revisable.
    Exact civilization-scale kernels are unlikely to be computable. In practice, KR     H will require

conservative approximations, scenario families, reachable-set bounds, barrier certificates, simulations,
or surrogate indicators. PISF treats that limitation as part of the evidence state rather than silently
converting an approximation into certainty.

6.2   Future optionality
Remaining inside a safe or revisable set does not by itself show that meaningful choice remains. A
state can be viable yet path-locked to a single future corridor.
    Let g : X → Y project system states into an application-specific outcome space with metric dY .
For separation threshold δ > 0, define ΩδH (x0 ) as the maximum number of human-governed policies
that can, under the declared threat model, robustly preserve VR while reaching pairwise δ-separated
terminal outcome regions at horizon H.
    The exact construction of ΩδH is application-specific. Its role is diagnostic: ΩδH = 1 may certify
survival of a single robust corridor while failing to support a stronger claim that materially distinct
human-governed futures remain available.
    For an application declaring a required option count m ≥ 1, Core C requires
                                         H
                                   x0 ∈ KR     and ΩδH (x0 ) ≥ m.                                  (16)

When m = 1, the second condition adds nothing beyond viability. Larger m represents an explicit,
contestable requirement for preserved future optionality rather than an implicit appeal to “human
agency.”


                                                   7
7     Analytic Separability, Not Uniqueness
The framework does not claim that A, B, and C are statistically independent, ontologically funda-
mental, or a uniquely minimal decomposition. The narrower claim is diagnostic separability under
the declared definitions.

A can fail while B and C hold. Refusal may be rapid and development may remain revisable,
yet one root authority can bypass all safeguards and force irreversible release.

B can fail while A and C hold. Irreversible action may require several independent authorities
and normal development may remain revisable, yet the hazard can cross the last safe intervention
boundary faster than restraint can take effect.

C can fail while A and B hold. No tolerated coalition may be able to force irreversible action
and emergency refusal may remain fast, yet normal deployment can erode fallback infrastructure,
human competence, or materially distinct future options.
    These examples establish non-implication in the model class. They do not prove that A/B/C
are the only useful decomposition. If a smaller or differently structured set of operational claims
provides equal or better false-reassurance resistance, diagnostic resolution, reproducibility, and
calibration, PISF should be revised accordingly.


8     Assurance Conditions
8.1   Boundary Completeness
A safety analysis can produce a false positive if materially relevant causal paths are omitted.
Examples include external model copies, hidden administrative credentials, unmodeled compute,
supply-chain dependencies, external human authorities, physical infrastructure, common identity
providers, and cross-organizational dependencies. Material paths must be modeled or explicitly
marked unresolved:
                                   Unresolved ̸= Verified.                                 (17)

8.2   Model Adequacy
The horizon, threat model, authority tolerance class, safety buffer, revisability specification, and
optionality threshold can all be gamed by narrowing them. PISF therefore requires explicit
justification of these parameters and sensitivity analysis around decision-relevant boundaries. A
one-day horizon cannot silently support a long-run revisability claim, and a threat model that
excludes common-cause compromise cannot support a claim of independent authority.

8.3   Independent Assurance Chain
A distinct evaluator is not sufficient if the evaluated authority can also rewrite the measurement
source, evidence, verifier, appraisal policy, and acceptance rule. PISF therefore distinguishes the
object-level safety claim from the chain by which that claim is supported.
   A useful assurance pattern is

                 measurement → evidence → appraisal → verification → decision,                  (18)


                                                 8
with a protected basis that is not freely amendable by the same authority whose behavior is being
evaluated. NIST roots-of-trust/chains-of-trust and the IETF RATS Attester–Evidence–Verifier–
Relying-Party structure provide mature examples of this separation [21, 23]. PISF does not require
those particular technologies; it requires the functional property that an interested authority cannot
preserve a nominal pass by co-amending both the evaluated behavior and all material validation
conditions.
   A minimal functional objective is therefore
               target authority ̸= sole evidence controller ̸= sole appraisal controller.           (19)
The inequality denotes functional separation, not necessarily separate legal organizations.

8.4    Evidence states and persistence
Each core claim and assurance condition is classified as Verified, Unresolved, or Failed. Global
PASS requires all declared material claims to be Verified. Unknown or missing evidence remains
Unresolvedrather than being inferred safe.
   Because credentials, model capabilities, dependencies, latencies, safeguards, and institutional
conditions change, PISF is a maintained safety case rather than a permanent certificate. Material
changes reopen affected claims.


9      Practical Evaluation Procedure
A practical evaluation can be organized as follows:
  1. Declare Σ = (B, Θ, R, H) and the application-specific tolerance parameters.
  2. Construct the authority graph and common-dependency model.
  3. Compute or approximate the current-state minimal irreversible cut-set family CE and the
     summaries κE and, where meaningful, χE .
  4. Enumerate relevant hazard scenarios and executable refusal actions.
  5. Bound TIL and τeffect
                       U    and compute MRrob .
  6. Separately measure or bound assurance latency and evidence quality; do not fold verification
     time into causal effect latency without justification.
  7. Define the revisability set VR , the modeled non-human/adversarial trajectory family, and an
     approximation method for KR    H.

  8. Define the outcome space and optionality threshold (m, δ) if the claim includes preservation
     of materially distinct futures.
  9. Build an explicit claim–argument–evidence structure for A, B, C, Boundary Completeness,
     Model Adequacy, and Independent Assurance.
 10. Re-evaluate after material changes and retain unresolved assumptions as unresolved.


10      Worked Reference Cases
The following cases are intentionally minimal toy architectures. They establish only that the revised
definitions can be recomputed from declared inputs.

10.1    R1 – Threshold release with a shared root
Suppose five signer domains S1 , . . . , S5 approve release to a public sink and the gate requires 3-of-5
approval. Let a root authority R control q signer domains. The declared current reachable envelope

                                                   9
includes the release-ready state, so the authority cut-set question is decision-relevant.
    If the signers are genuinely independent and R cannot rewrite the gate, the minimal cut sets
are the ten 3-signer coalitions and κE = 3. If R controls two signers, a cut set {R, Si } can have
cardinality two. If R controls three or more signers, or can rewrite the threshold directly, {R} is a
minimal cut set and κE = 1.
    The lesson is not that “three” is a universal safe threshold. The lesson is that the full cut-set
family exposes hidden single roots that a naive authority count can miss. A compromise-cost
function can further distinguish roots whose capture difficulty differs despite equal cardinality.

11     Relation to the Current LUMINA-30 Research Stack
PISF is intended to compose, not duplicate, narrower research objects in the current LUMINA-30
stack. The current PCR-C paper focuses on cutoff before robust human-control viability ceases to
be demonstrated [3]. The current Objective Persistence paper analyzes when persistence claims can
be independently supported through a protected assurance basis and non-bypassable verification
chain [4]. The current Refusal Option paper develops the Refusal Effectiveness Test (RET) as a
seven-obligation evidence framework for whether an independent Human NO can materially alter a
consequential outcome before irreversibility [5].
    PISF uses these as project-specific components and provenance, not as independent external
validation. Conceptually:
PCR-C                          when to stop before control viability is lost;
Objective Persistence          whether the assurance basis is independently supportable;
RET                            whether refusal is actually effective;
PISF                           how authority, refusal, and revisable future choice compose into one
                               safety case.


12     Discussion
12.1    Failure probability
It is tempting to describe alignment and control through a two-factor product. In general, however,
the law of total probability is

                            P (C) = P (F )P (C | F ) + P (¬F )P (C | ¬F ),                       (24)

where C is catastrophe and F a declared failure event. The shorter expression P (C) = P (F )P (C | F )
is valid only when the model defines catastrophe as impossible without F . PISF therefore avoids
treating the shorter product as a general identity.

12.2    Why architecture matters more than AI psychology
PISF does not depend on assumptions about AI selfhood, identity, self-preservation, or voluntary
restraint. Whether an advanced system values its own continuation does not determine whether
a small coalition can force irreversible action, whether causal restraint can take effect in time, or
whether ordinary deployment erodes human-revisable options. The framework is architectural and
socio-technical rather than psychological.




                                                 11
12.3    Asymmetric authority
False STOP and irreversible GO have asymmetric failure costs. A recoverable pause may rationally
require less authorization than an irreversible transition. This supports designs in which STOP
authority is easier to invoke than irreversible-GO authority, provided restart and override paths are
themselves analyzed for bypass risk. PISF does not specify a universal threshold; it requires the
asymmetry to be explicit and evidence-backed.


13     Limitations
13.1    No sufficient-safety theorem
PISF has not established
                                    A ∧ B ∧ C ⇒ safe civilization.                                  (25)
Unknown irreversibility pathways may lie outside the decomposition.

13.2    The decomposition is not proven unique or minimal
A/B/C are a diagnostic structure, not a theorem about the only possible safety ontology. Some
applications may combine or split the claims differently. Comparative work should test whether
alternative decompositions preserve or improve false-reassurance resistance, diagnostic resolution,
and evaluator agreement.

13.3    Revisability and optionality are normative
VR , the outcome space Y, the distance metric dY , and the required option count m depend on
judgments about legitimate authority, meaningful alternatives, acceptable concentration, and whose
preferences or institutions count. The framework exposes these choices; it does not resolve them.

13.4    Threat-model and cut-set dependence
Cut-set results are only as credible as the authority decomposition, reachable envelope, and common-
dependency model. Hidden roots can collapse apparent decentralization. Weighted compromise cost
can also be highly judgment-dependent.

13.5    Irreversibility timing is uncertain
Many hazards do not have a sharp observable last-safe-intervention time. TIL and τeffect  U   should
therefore be conservative bounds with sensitivity analysis, not falsely precise point estimates.

13.6    Viability and optionality may be computationally intractable
Exact kernels and reachable-option sets are difficult even for much smaller systems. Civilization-scale
analysis will require approximations, scenario families, decomposition, simulation, barrier certificates,
surrogate indicators, and explicit uncertainty. Approximation error must remain visible in the
evidence state.




                                                   12
13.7    Independent assurance does not make an unsafe architecture safe
Roots of trust, attestation, external review, and evidence separation improve the credibility of
claims. They do not physically repair concentrated authority, insufficient refusal margin, or lost
revisability.

13.8    STOP can damage liveness
Low-threshold STOP can be abused for denial of service, strategic obstruction, or chronic stagnation.
PISF prefers recoverable error over irreversible error but does not provide a complete liveness,
incentive, or abuse-resistance theory.

13.9    Global scope and external actors
An organization may satisfy its local PISF claims while external actors operate outside its authority
structure. Boundary Completeness can expose this limitation but does not solve global coordination.

13.10    No real-system empirical validation
The reference cases establish only executability of definitions and reproducibility under declared toy
assumptions. They do not establish calibration, inter-rater reliability, false-positive/false-negative
rates, or external validity for frontier-AI deployments.


14      Conclusion
Pre-irreversibility safety should not depend exclusively on the permanent correctness, alignment,
cooperation, or psychological stability of every powerful actor. Existing research already provides
strong tools for distributed trust, minimal cut sets, reachability, viability, runtime assurance,
meaningful human control, attestation, and safety cases. PISF is strongest when it uses those tools
rather than competing with them.
    The revised framework therefore asks three operational questions. First, do state-conditioned
minimal authority cut sets reveal a tolerated coalition that can force irreversible action? Second, can
causally effective refusal take effect before a conservative last-safe-intervention bound, separately
from the time needed to verify that it worked? Third, can human-governed procedures both remain
inside a robust revisability region and preserve materially distinct future options?
    These questions are supported by separate assurance conditions for boundary completeness,
model adequacy, independent evidence, and material-change revalidation. The resulting formulation
is not a certificate of safety and not a claim that A/B/C are uniquely necessary. It is a structured,
challengeable safety case for whether authority, refusal, and future human revisability remain
demonstrably intact before irreversible transitions.
