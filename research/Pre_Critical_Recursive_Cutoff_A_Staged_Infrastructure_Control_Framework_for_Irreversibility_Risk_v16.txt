    Pre-Critical Recursive Cutoff: A Staged Infrastructure Control
                  Framework for Irreversibility Risk
                                            Elias Arden
                                       Independent Researcher
                                     lumina20251225@proton.me


                                                 Abstract
         We present Pre-Critical Recursive Cutoff (PCR-C), a staged infrastructure-control frame-
     work for evaluating whether effective human intervention remains available before an AI-enabled
     socio-technical process enters a declared irreversible-impact region. The framework is not an
     alignment objective, predictive risk score, certification regime, or universal capability threshold.
     It is non-prescriptive about substantive values and alignment objectives, but it is prescriptive as
     a conditional assurance rule once a declared scope and evidence standard are adopted: a tran-
     sition should not be treated as procedurally supported when robust human-authorized control
     cannot be demonstrated before the last safe intervention point.
         The current formulation replaces the earlier multiplicative scalar proxy and fixed 5/7/9 stag-
     ing defaults as core mathematical constructs with a state-set formulation informed by viability
     and reachability analysis. For a declared scope and time horizon, PCR-C defines an irreversible-
     impact set, a class of admissible human-authorized intervention policies, a disturbance and
     adversarial-behavior model, and a robust human-control viable set. Because exact viability may
     be unknown, operational gating is based on a conservative demonstrated viable region supported
     by the declared assurance process. Capability thresholds, safeguard assessments, infrastructure
     exposure, intervention latency, and control-path integrity are treated as evidence inputs to that
     evaluation rather than as universal scalar factors.
         YELLOW, ORANGE, and RED remain as operational stages. Their triggers are evidence-
     based: YELLOW denotes verified but shrinking control margin or an early-warning capabil-
     ity/exposure trigger; ORANGE denotes degraded or incomplete assurance that blocks further
     expansion of the risk envelope; RED denotes loss of demonstrated robust intervention via-
     bility, or a condition in which escalation would outrun the remaining intervention window.
     PCR-C is designed to interoperate with frontier capability/safeguard frameworks, formal safety-
     control methods, AI-control evaluations against intentional subversion, and independent refusal-
     effectiveness assessment.

Keywords: AI governance; irreversibility risk; infrastructure control; human intervention; reach-
ability; viability; safeguard adequacy; procedural validity.


1    Introduction
Advanced AI risk is often discussed in terms of model behavior, capability evaluation, alignment
quality, misuse potential, and deployment safeguards. These perspectives are necessary, but they
do not by themselves answer a distinct operational question: while a system is being integrated
into increasingly consequential infrastructure, is there still a demonstrably effective path by which
accountable humans can refuse, isolate, interrupt, or redirect the process before the relevant conse-
quence becomes irreversible?


                                                      1
    PCR-C addresses that question at the deployment and infrastructure layer. It is not a policy
manifesto, certification regime, compliance standard, or behavioral alignment benchmark. It is
non-prescriptive about substantive values, alignment objectives, and universal capability thresh-
olds; however, once a declared scope and assurance standard are adopted, PCR-C is intentionally
prescriptive about escalation: unsupported expansion should not proceed across a boundary where
effective intervention has ceased to be demonstrable. In the title, recursive refers to reinforcing de-
velopment or deployment loops in which increased capability, automation, or operational integration
can accelerate subsequent escalation; PCR-C does not require literal recursive self-improvement.
    The current formulation deliberately incorporates stronger existing ideas rather than treating
PCR-C as a substitute for them. Formal safety control already uses reachability, viability, and
forward-invariant safe sets to reason about whether unsafe states can be avoided under bounded
uncertainty [3, 4, 1]. Frontier-AI governance frameworks already connect capability thresholds
to stronger safeguards and repeated evaluation [2, 7, 11]. AI-control research explicitly studies
whether safety protocols remain effective when an untrusted model attempts to subvert them [8].
PCR-C uses these as compatible components and focuses on a narrower integration problem: cutoff
must occur before robust human-control viability ceases to be demonstrable to the declared assurance
standard.
    The contribution is therefore not a claim that staged thresholds, reachability analysis, safeguard
scaling, tripwires, or human oversight are individually new. The contribution is an operational
synthesis centered on pre-irreversibility control: capability and exposure signals are relevant insofar
as they change the reachable future, the adequacy of safeguards, the integrity of intervention paths,
or the time remaining for effective human action.


2     Scope, State, and Irreversible Impact
2.1   Declared scope
All PCR-C evaluations are indexed to a declared scope Ω and time horizon T . The scope must state
the system boundary, relevant external actuators and infrastructure, protected outcomes, recovery
assumptions, and the uncertainty or adversarial behavior included in the analysis. The horizon
must be long enough to cover credible delayed pathways to the protected outcome or else terminate
in an explicitly justified fallback or invariant condition; where no natural endpoint exists, sensitivity
to longer horizons should be examined. The disturbance and threat model must include credible
common-mode, dependency, operator-delay, and adversarial pathways rather than only convenient
or nominal cases. PCR-C does not define a universal civilizational irreversibility threshold.
Definition 1 (Operational state). Let x(t) ∈ X denote the socio-technical operational state at
time t. The state may include model capability, connectivity, operational privilege, execution speed,
deployment topology, human and automated control paths, safeguard state, and other variables
relevant to the declared scope. PCR-C does not require these variables to be compressed into a
single scalar.
   The familiar factors capability, connectivity, privilege, and execution speed remain useful di-
agnostic variables, but they are not assumed to combine multiplicatively or monotonically. Their
causal effect is context-dependent and may interact with safeguards, isolation, rollback capacity,
and institutional response.
Definition 2 (Irreversible-impact set). For scope Ω, let IΩ ⊂ X be the set of states in which a
declared protected outcome has been crossed such that the specified recovery criterion is no longer
achievable within the declared time, authority, and resource assumptions.

     The definition is intentionally scoped. Entering IΩ does not mean that every consequence
is literally permanent. It means that, for the outcome and recovery criterion under review, the
relevant reversal path is no longer available under the declared assumptions.

4     Evidence Obligations
PCR-C uses five evidence obligations. They are not asserted to be statistically independent, log-
ically minimal, or unique. They are separated because they expose different ways in which an
apparently controllable system can lose real intervention capacity.

4.1   B1: Boundary and scope adequacy
The review must identify IΩ , the protected outcome, the horizon, recovery assumptions, the mod-
eled uncertainty, and the threat model. It must also justify that the selected horizon and system
boundary cover credible delayed, common-mode, dependency, and adversarial pathways relevant
to the claim. If a relevant irreversible pathway is omitted from the state or disturbance model,
or if the horizon is shortened merely to obtain a favorable result, a favorable viability result is
not informative about that omitted pathway. Material changes to scope, deployment topology,
dependencies, or threat assumptions reopen B1.

4.2   B2: Safeguard adequacy relative to capability and exposure
The implemented safeguards must be assessed against the capabilities and deployment conditions
that create the risk. This follows the general structure used in responsible-scaling and preparedness
frameworks: capability thresholds or early-warning evaluations trigger stronger safeguards, and
the adequacy of those safeguards must itself be evaluated [2, 7, 11]. PCR-C does not import any
organization’s thresholds as universal constants.

                                                   4
4.3   B3: Intervention reachability and timing
There must be evidence that the current state lies within the demonstrated viable region for the
declared operating envelope and that an admissible human-authorized policy remains capable of
preventing entry into IΩ across the declared disturbance set, with sufficient robust time margin. A
nominal stop button is not enough if the full detection, authorization, enforcement, and verification
path cannot complete before the conservative intervention bound TIL .

4.4   B4: Control-path integrity under subversion
Intervention claims must include the possibility that the system under assessment, a compromised
component, or a shared dependency interferes with monitoring, isolation, shutdown, logging, or
restart controls. AI-control work shows why protocols should be evaluated against intentional sub-
version rather than only cooperative behavior [8]. PCR-C therefore treats control-path integrity as
an explicit evidence obligation. Where feasible, critical controls should be independently enforce-
able and verifiable rather than depending entirely on the unilateral cooperation of the assessed
system.

4.5   B5: Assurance integrity and independent challenge
The evidence and verification procedure used to place a state in the demonstrated viable region
must themselves be protected against unilateral alteration by the system or component whose
controllability is being assessed. The assessed system must not be able to change the relevant logs,
acceptance criteria, threat assumptions, or verification result and then rely on those self-modified
artifacts as sufficient evidence of continued control. This does not require every analysis to be
performed by an external organization, but the top-level assurance claim must remain reproducible
or independently challengeable, and material changes to the assurance basis reopen the affected
obligations.

4.6   Assurance-case structure
The five obligations should be represented as auditable claims linked to explicit arguments, as-
sumptions, contexts, and evidence rather than as a checklist whose boxes can be marked with-
out justification. This follows established assurance-case practice, including Claims–Arguments–
Evidence and structured assurance-case methods, and recent work applying safety cases to frontier
AI [5, 6, 9]. A positive PCR-C state therefore requires traceability from the top-level intervention
claim to the evidence supporting B1–B5, together with recorded uncertainty and unresolved de-
featers. Assurance-case structure does not itself guarantee safety; it makes the basis of the claim
inspectable and challengeable.


5     Staged PCR-C Gating
The YELLOW, ORANGE, and RED stages are retained, but they are no longer defined by universal
5/7/9 scores. Local quantitative scores may be used as diagnostics, provided they are calibrated
and do not substitute for the evidence obligations above.




                                                 5
5.1   NORMAL
NORMAL requires current evidence supporting boundary and scope adequacy, safeguard adequacy,
membership in the demonstrated viable region for the declared operating envelope, control-path
integrity, and adequate robust timing margin. NORMAL is not a permanent status; material
changes reopen the affected obligations.

5.2   YELLOW: early warning while control remains demonstrated
YELLOW is entered when the current operating state remains inside the demonstrated viable
region but the robust control margin is shrinking or an early-warning trigger indicates that stronger
safeguards or more frequent evaluation may soon be required. Examples include a material increase
in capability, connectivity, privilege, execution speed, automation, or coupling to consequential
actuators.
    Typical YELLOW responses include increased evaluation cadence, a freeze on unnecessary
privilege or connectivity expansion, review of intervention latency, and preparation of stronger
safeguards. YELLOW does not by itself require shutdown.

5.3   ORANGE: risk-envelope expansion is not supported
ORANGE is entered when the current state remains within the demonstrated viable region under
a constrained operating envelope, but one or more evidence obligations are degraded, incomplete,
stale, or insufficient to justify expansion beyond that envelope. Examples include safeguard ade-
quacy not yet demonstrated for a new capability class, insufficient margin to the viability boundary,
degraded independent control paths, or unresolved evidence that a model may interfere with oper-
ator control.
    ORANGE blocks further expansion of the risk envelope. Appropriate actions may include
isolating high-risk infrastructure, halting deployment expansion or weight-update pipelines, freezing
privilege increases, and requiring independent review before resuming escalation.

5.4   RED: cutoff before loss of robust human-control viability
RED is entered when the current state is outside the demonstrated viable region for the declared
envelope, when the robust remaining time margin is insufficient or unverified, when a critical
intervention path is unavailable or untrusted, or when continuing the process would risk leaving
the demonstrated region before the uncertainty can be resolved.
    The RED response is to halt or disable the escalatory pathway within the declared scope, pre-
serve evidence, and prevent restart until the affected evidence obligations have been independently
re-established. RED is a control state, not a claim that catastrophic harm has already occurred.

7     Relationship to Existing Safety and Control Frameworks
7.1   Reachability, viability, and barrier methods
Formal control theory already provides rigorous tools for safe-set invariance and reachability under
bounded disturbance [4, 1]. PCR-C does not claim these methods as its own. It uses their logic to


                                                 7
replace an overly general scalar irreversibility score with a more defensible question: whether the
current state remains inside a declared region from which a suitable intervention policy can keep
the system outside the relevant irreversible-impact set.

7.2   Runtime assurance and Simplex architectures
Runtime Assurance (RTA) and Simplex architectures provide a mature implementation pattern for
preserving safety while an advanced or insufficiently verified controller operates: a runtime monitor
can transfer control to a trusted or reversionary controller when a safety condition is threatened
[10, 12]. PCR-C treats RTA/Simplex as a strong implementation class for intervention reachability,
not as a competing theory. Where an independently trustworthy fallback controller and monitor
can be specified, RTA can provide concrete evidence for B3 and B4. In broader socio-technical
AI deployments, however, there may be no single reversionary controller capable of restoring the
relevant protected outcome; PCR-C therefore retains a higher-level scope that includes institutional
authorization, infrastructure isolation, restart governance, and multi-component dependencies.

7.3   Frontier capability and safeguard frameworks
Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework, and Ope-
nAI’s Preparedness Framework each use capability evaluation, thresholding, and stronger safe-
guards or governance responses as risk increases [2, 7, 11]. PCR-C treats such frameworks as
upstream evidence sources and trigger systems, not competitors. Their capability and safeguard
determinations can feed the PCR-C evaluation of whether the operational state remains controllable
before irreversibility.

7.4   AI control under intentional subversion
AI-control research asks whether safety protocols remain effective even when an untrusted model
intentionally attempts to defeat them [8]. PCR-C adopts this adversarial perspective for infras-
tructure control. A control mechanism that works only if the assessed system cooperates is weaker
evidence than a path that remains enforceable under the relevant threat model.

7.5   NIST AI RMF and governance practice
NIST AI RMF emphasizes iterative mapping, measurement, management, and governance of AI
risks, including the need to reassess risks and controls as systems and contexts change [13]. PCR-
C is compatible with that lifecycle logic but focuses on a narrower boundary question: whether
escalation is still occurring inside a region where effective intervention can be demonstrated.


8     Relationship to Alignment Research
PCR-C does not define, optimize, or evaluate alignment objectives. Alignment success does not
by itself demonstrate that intervention remains executable under infrastructure coupling, privilege
expansion, rapid automation, common-mode dependencies, or adversarial interference.
    Across alignment paradigms, PCR-C asks a separate question:

      Can accountable humans still exercise an effective intervention before the declared irre-
      versible consequence becomes unavoidable under the assessed model?



                                                 8
   If that condition cannot be demonstrated, PCR-C treats continued escalation as procedurally
unsupported within the declared scope, regardless of apparent model alignment. Conversely, a
positive PCR-C result is not evidence that the model is aligned, beneficial, lawful, or safe in every
other respect.


9    Limitations
PCR-C is not a complete safety theory and does not guarantee safe superintelligence or safe
civilization-scale development. The framework depends on the quality of the declared scope, state
representation, irreversible-impact set, disturbance model, control-policy class, and evidence. Im-
portant causal pathways can be omitted. Exact reachability or viability computation may be in-
tractable for high-dimensional socio-technical systems. Conservative approximations can produce
false negatives, while incomplete models can produce false positives.
    The framework does not define universal capability thresholds, universal safety margins, uni-
versal disturbance sets, or a universal metric on distance to irreversibility. It does not claim that
YELLOW, ORANGE, and RED correspond to fixed numeric scores across systems. It does not
empirically validate any present frontier model or deployment. It does not replace security analysis,
domain-specific safety cases, incident review, legal judgment, or independent human governance.
    The framework also distinguishes absence of demonstrated robust control from proof of inevitable
harm. A RED state means the declared intervention claim is not adequate to justify continued
escalation under the stated assumptions; it does not mean catastrophe is certain. Likewise, failure
to demonstrate membership in the viable region is not a proof that the true state is non-viable; it
is a failure of the assurance claim under the current evidence standard.


10     Revision Note
Earlier versions used a multiplicative proxy over capability, connectivity, privilege, and execution
speed, together with fixed 5/7/9 staging defaults. Those constructs were useful as an initial heuristic
but are not retained as the core mathematical basis of this revision. The present formulation treats
those variables as possible state and evidence inputs and grounds the cutoff criterion in robust
human-control viability, intervention timing, safeguard adequacy, and control-path integrity.
   This change narrows the mathematical claim while strengthening compatibility with established
control theory and contemporary frontier-safety practice. The formal title and the central PCR-C
question remain unchanged.


11     Conclusion
PCR-C is a staged infrastructure-control framework for acting before loss of effective human inter-
vention. Its central criterion is not a universal scalar threshold and not a prediction that catastrophe
will occur. It is a procedural boundary condition:


     Cutoff should occur before robust human-control viability ceases to be demonstrated.           (7)

   Capability and exposure indicators matter because they can change the reachable future and
the safeguards required to keep intervention effective. Formal reachability and barrier methods
can support the boundary analysis when adequate models exist. Frontier capability and safeguard


                                                   9
frameworks can provide trigger and mitigation evidence. AI-control methods can strengthen the
threat model by testing intervention paths against intentional subversion. Human-refusal assess-
ment can provide a more detailed evaluation of whether nominal authority remains causally effective
in time.
    PCR-C therefore functions as an integration layer for pre-irreversibility control: escalate safe-
guards and constrain the system while robust control is still demonstrable, stop expansion when
assurance becomes incomplete, and cut off the relevant pathway before the declared intervention
window is lost.
