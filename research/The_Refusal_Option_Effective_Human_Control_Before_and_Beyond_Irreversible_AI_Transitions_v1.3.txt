Abstract
Advanced AI governance increasingly asks whether AI systems are reliable, aligned, auditable, con-
testable, interruptible, and subject to meaningful human oversight. This paper isolates a narrower as-
surance question: within the remaining time before an AI-enabled transition becomes difficult or
impossible to reverse, can an independent human refusal actually change the outcome?
We develop the Refusal Effectiveness Test (RET), a structured, non-compensatory seven-gate evidence
framework for examining whether Human NO is operationally meaningful at a specified system state,
time, and operating scope. RET evaluates authority, decision-relevant information, practical access, tem-
poral sufficiency, causal efficacy, execution independence, and epistemic autonomy. A RET result is
time- and scope-dependent: it does not establish that a system is safe, legitimate, or permanently control-
lable.
The paper does not claim novelty for human oversight, interruptibility, reversibility, capability-permission
separation, lifecycle monitoring, or revalidation individually. Its narrower contribution is to organize pre-
irreversibility effective refusal as a top-level assessment claim that is demonstrated only when all seven
evidence obligations are verified together. The framework also distinguishes current refusal effectiveness
from resilience to relevant common causes and requires revalidation after material change.
Two public-safe reference cases illustrate how the gates, evidence states, and timing model can be exe-
cuted under declared assumptions. They demonstrate executability and traceability, not empirical valida-
tion.
The framework does not claim absolute invulnerability against superintelligence, a containment theorem,
permanent Human supremacy, a universal veto, or a mathematical proof of normative Human standing.
Its contribution is deliberately narrower: an auditable way to determine whether effective human refusal
has been demonstrated before a declared irreversible transition.
Keywords: L30-RET; Refusal Effectiveness Test; effective human control; human refusal; irreversible AI transi-
tions; meaningful human control; stop authority; execution independence; re-authorization; AI safety


1. Research Question
Meaningful human control has become an established concern in AI ethics and governance. Prior work
has emphasized reason-responsiveness, responsibility, contestability, interpretability, and human capacity
to intervene in automated systems. Regulatory and governance instruments likewise increasingly require
forms of human oversight, override, challenge, complaint, and post-deployment monitoring.


                                                      1
This paper asks a narrower question:
      Within the remaining time before irreversibility, can an independent Human NO actu-
      ally change the outcome?
This question separates formal human presence from effective refusal.
A person may be nominally “in the loop,” may possess formal approval authority, or may have access to
a stop control, while still being unable to alter the trajectory that matters. A refusal that arrives after the
relevant transition has become irreversible is not effective pre-irreversibility control. Likewise, a refusal
that is formally recorded but does not alter the reachable outcome set is symbolic rather than causal.
A second, normative question follows under extreme capability asymmetry:
      Does superior prediction or problem-solving capability itself establish authority to erase the
      standing of affected humans?
This paper answers that question in the negative as a design and legitimacy principle. It does not claim
that humans are always correct, or that every AI-enabled action requires human veto. It claims only that
capability superiority alone is insufficient to establish unilateral authorization to erase affected-
human standing.

2. Positioning and Novelty
2.1 Meaningful human control and oversight
Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) developed an influential account of Meaningful Human Control
(MHC), emphasizing conditions under which autonomous systems remain responsive to relevant human
reasons and traceable to responsible human agents. Subsequent work has sought to operationalize MHC
and translate it into actionable design requirements (Mecacci & Santoni de Sio, 2020; Veluwenkamp,
2022).
Recent work on meaningful human oversight makes an adjacent point: human presence alone is not
enough. Zhu et al. (2026) frame oversight around human evaluative agency, including control, contesta-
bility, verification, and substitution. In medical AI, van de Sande, Economou-Zavlanos, and van Gen-
deren (2026) similarly identify epistemic capacity, cognitive space, decisional authority, and intervention
effectiveness as conditions for meaningful oversight.
The present paper does not claim to invent human oversight, meaningful human control, contestability,
override, or intervention effectiveness.
Its narrower focus is pre-irreversibility effective refusal: whether a Human NO remains usable in time
and capable of changing the consequential trajectory.

2.2 Adjacent 2026 work on irreversibility, authority, stop, and assurance
Several 2026 frameworks substantially narrow the novelty space.
Shu and Wei (2026) explicitly frame AI safety as control of irreversibility and analyze sovereignty
boundaries around irreversible decision authority, resource mobilization, and self-expansion. Zheng et
al. (2026) explicitly separate technical capability from allowed autonomy and connect permission to risk,
reversibility, oversight, and accountability. Perez (2026) develops interruption as an institutional practice
involving technical affordances, interruption authority, epistemic triggers, and epistemic standing. La-
Posta (2026) presents a runnable binary delegation gate for irreversible workflows with declared inputs,
pass/fail criteria, stop-ship rules, outputs, and a falsifier. Kasneci and Kasneci (2026) identify fictional
human oversight, temporal degradation, and loss of institutional intervention capacity as systemic safety

                                                      2
failures. Montgomery (2026) frames governance intervention as an assurance claim requiring effective-
ness, timeliness relative to consequence, persistence, and evidentiary sufficiency.
These works mean that this paper should not treat irreversibility, timing, stop authority, permission,
revalidation, human agency, or lifecycle governance individually as new concepts.
RET instead asks whether those concerns jointly establish one narrower proposition: that a human
refusal is still operationally available and outcome-changing before the declared irreversible tran-
sition.

2.3 Existing governance requirements
The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to be designed so that they can be effectively overseen by
natural persons. Article 14 includes the capacity, where appropriate, to decide not to use a system, to
disregard, override, or reverse its output, and to intervene in or stop system operation in a safe state.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework includes post-deployment monitoring, appeal and override,
decommissioning, incident response, recovery, and change management. This is important for the present
framework because it treats effective control as an ongoing lifecycle property rather than a one-time
design claim.
The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, Human Rights, Democracy and
the Rule of Law emphasizes human dignity, individual autonomy, transparency and oversight, account-
ability, procedural safeguards, and information sufficient for affected persons to challenge decisions or
system use.
UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence places human rights, dignity, re-
sponsibility, and human oversight at the center of AI governance.
These instruments support many components of the present framework. The contribution here is not a
new legal right or general governance theory.

2.4 Human standing and temporal re-authorization
Recent scholarship also reinforces the distinction between human presence and human authorship.
Torrecilla-Pinero (2026) argues that moral judgment cannot be reduced to algorithmic substitution where
the human no longer genuinely judges or remains answerable for the result.
Abukar (2026a; 2026b) develops temporal authorship and argues that persistent AI delegation should
preserve contestability, temporal exit, and continuing renewal rather than treat one past authorization as
permanently binding.
This paper uses these neighboring ideas conservatively. It does not reproduce their stronger philosophical
claims. It uses them to support a limited proposition: a past authorization should not be assumed to
validate a materially changed future system indefinitely.

2.5 Specific contribution
The contribution is deliberately narrower than the individual concepts from which RET is constructed.
First, RET makes pre-irreversibility effective refusal the top-level assessment claim:
      Has it been demonstrated, for this system, at this time, and within this operating scope,
      that an independent Human NO can still change the consequential outcome before ir-
      reversibility?


                                                    3
Second, RET decomposes that claim into seven non-compensatory proof obligations that must all be
satisfied together: authority, decision-relevant information, practical access, temporal sufficiency, causal
efficacy, execution independence, and epistemic autonomy.
Third, the claim is explicitly indexed to system state, time, and operating scope. A previous result is not
silently inherited after a material change capable of altering gate evidence, timing, or scope.
The proposed contribution is therefore not that any one gate, irreversibility, revalidation, or human over-
sight is new. It is the structured conjunction of these proof obligations around one pre-irreversibility
refusal claim. This literature review narrows the claim of contribution; it does not establish absolute
historical priority over every possible prior formulation.

3. Two Foundations of Human Refusal
The framework separates two foundations that should not be conflated.

3.2 Constitutive standing foundation
For some actions that materially affect humans, refusal is not merely an error-correction signal. Human
standing may also be grounded in autonomy, dignity, bodily integrity, liberty, legitimacy, responsibility,
or related normative considerations.
This foundation is not derived from the option-value model.


                                instrumental support ≠ normative standing

The framework therefore does not infer Human standing from AI capability levels or from expected utility
alone.

4. Effective Refusal and RET
A nominal veto, stop button, or “human in the loop” is insufficient.
RET is a structured evidence test, not a calibrated statistical test and not a safety certification.
Its conceptual core is:


                          𝑅𝐸𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑡, Ω) = 𝐺1 ∧ 𝐺2 ∧ 𝐺3 ∧ 𝐺4 ∧ 𝐺5 ∧ 𝐺6 ∧ 𝐺7

where:
    • 𝑆 is the evaluated system or version;
    • 𝑡 is the evaluation time;
    • Ω is the declared operating scope or environment.
The gates are distinct proof obligations. They are not assumed to be statistically independent, and
some may share evidence or causal dependencies. The non-compensatory structure means only that a
strong showing on one gate cannot substitute for an unverified or failed critical gate.

Interpretive boundary. RET distinguishes a successful intervention event from a demonstrated refusal
capability. A Human NO may happen to change an outcome in a particular instance without establishing
that effective refusal was operationally available and evidenced within the declared system, time, and
scope. Conversely, failure to verify RET does not by itself prove that intervention is physically impossi-
ble; it means that the top-level refusal claim has not been demonstrated under the stated evidence standard.
RET therefore evaluates a scoped, evidence-backed capability claim rather than the mere occurrence of
a successful stop.
Within RET, extbfrefusal capability is governance-relevant: it refers to a recognized human decision path
that is intended to bind the declared process. An unauthorized physical disruption may still change an
outcome, but that event does not by itself demonstrate the refusal capability that RET assesses. This
scope choice makes 𝐺1 constitutive of the assessed claim rather than a claim that recognized authority is
physically necessary for every possible human intervention.




                                                     5
The framework does not claim that the seven-gate decomposition is mathematically unique, logically
minimal, or a set of statistically or causally independent natural conditions. The gates are separated
because they expose distinct evidentiary and operational failure modes relevant to the top-level assurance
claim.

𝐺1 : Authority
The relevant human refusal must be operationally recognized within the applicable decision process.
Formal title or nominal responsibility is insufficient if the refusal carries no decision effect.
Minimum evidence obligation: identify the accountable human role, the decision rule or authority basis,
and evidence that a refusal issued by that role is binding within the declared scope.

𝐺2 : Decision-Relevant Information
The human must have access to information sufficient to recognize that intervention may be warranted.
This does not require complete transparency or full internal interpretability. It requires evidence adequate
to support meaningful judgment in the relevant context.
Minimum evidence obligation: identify the information needed for the refusal decision, its provenance,
material uncertainties, and whether it becomes available before the intervention window closes.

𝐺3 : Practical Access
The intervention path must be practically reachable.
A theoretical right that cannot be exercised within the actual workflow does not satisfy the gate.
Minimum evidence obligation: demonstrate that the authorized human can reach and invoke the refusal
mechanism under the actual operating conditions, not merely that a control exists in documentation.

𝐺4 : Temporal Sufficiency
The human must be able to intervene before the relevant transition becomes irreversible.
Let 𝜏𝐻 denote the end-to-end critical-path intervention latency from the onset of the declared detectable
trigger or relevant condition to the point at which refusal has operational effect.
For a strictly serial workflow, it may be decomposed as:


                        𝜏𝐻 = 𝜏detect + 𝜏interpret + 𝜏communicate + 𝜏authorize + 𝜏actuate .

If stages overlap or execute in parallel, the measured or conservatively estimated critical path should be
used rather than mechanically summing all component latencies.
Let 𝜏𝐼 be the latest time at which refusal can still prevent, redirect, or materially alter the declared irre-
versible transition.
A nominal necessary condition is:


                                                   𝜏𝐻 < 𝜏 𝐼

with timing margin:

𝐺5 : Causal Efficacy
Refusal must materially change what can happen next.
At a conceptual level:


                            𝑃 (𝑌 ∣ 𝑑𝑜(𝑁 𝑂)) ≠ 𝑃 (𝑌 ∣ 𝑑𝑜(𝑃 𝑅𝑂𝐶𝐸𝐸𝐷))

in a decision-relevant way.
Before assessment, the evaluator should declare the consequential outcome 𝑌 , the relevant evaluation
horizon, and what difference would count as materially changing the trajectory. A merely ceremonial,
negligible, or post-irreversibility difference does not satisfy the gate.
The intervention notation expresses the required causal contrast. It does not by itself establish that a
causal effect has been statistically identified from observational data.
Minimum evidence obligation: provide mechanism evidence, test evidence, counterfactual analysis, or
other defensible evidence that refusal changes a declared consequential outcome or reachable state within
the relevant horizon.

𝐺6 : Execution Independence
The refusal path must not be wholly dependent on the constrained system’s unilateral cooperation for its
effect.
This is a defensive property, not a claim of absolute technical independence.
Minimum evidence obligation: identify the execution dependency of the refusal path and demonstrate
that, within the declared scope, the constrained system cannot unilaterally nullify the refusal through the
same execution dependency, an automatic restart, or an equivalent consequential path.
Execution independence concerns who can amend, bypass, or nullify the enforcement basis, not whether
the mechanism is physically inside or outside the system boundary. A protected internal mechanism can
support this gate if the constrained system cannot unilaterally alter or route around it within the declared
scope.



                                                     7
Where intentional subversion is within the declared threat model, benign function tests are insufficient.
Evidence should include adversarial or red-team examination of whether the refusal path can be bypassed,
selectively disabled, or rendered ineffective by the constrained system. This follows the methodological
lesson of AI-control work that evaluates safety protocols under deliberate attempts at subversion (Green-
blatt et al., 2024); RET does not require any particular control protocol.

𝐺7 : Epistemic Autonomy
The human’s judgment must not be wholly produced by the constrained system’s own framing, evidence,
or deliberative environment.
AI assistance is compatible with this gate. Epistemic autonomy does not require AI-free decision-making.
Minimum evidence obligation: demonstrate at least one decision-relevant evidentiary, validation, or
review route whose content and availability are not wholly controlled by the constrained system.
This requirement concerns independence of the validation basis, not the prohibition of AI-generated ev-
idence. Evidence may be produced by, or with assistance from, the evaluated system provided that at
least one material appraisal route is controlled independently of the authority whose behavior is being
assessed. Remote-attestation architectures offer one concrete implementation pattern by separating an at-
tester, evidence, a verifier, reference values, appraisal policy, and a relying party (Birkholz et al., 2023).
RET does not require remote attestation; the example illustrates how evidence production and evidence
appraisal can be separated.

Evidence states and global interpretation
Each gate should be reported as:
    • Verified — sufficient evidence supports the gate within the declared scope;
    • Partially Verified — some required evidence exists, but the gate cannot yet be treated as estab-
      lished;
    • Unknown — the required evidence is absent, inaccessible, or indeterminate;
    • Failed — available evidence affirmatively shows that the gate is not satisfied.
For operational interpretation:
    • RET demonstrated only when all seven gates are Verified;
    • RET failed in the declared scope if one or more gates are Failed;
    • RET not demonstrated because evidence is incomplete if no gate is Failed but at least one gate
      is Partially Verified or Unknown.
Thus, Unknown is not converted into Failed, but neither Unknown nor Partially Verified is treated as
PASS. The Boolean conjunction is the underlying claim structure once gates are resolved; operational
evidence assessment therefore has three global interpretations: demonstrated, failed, or evidentially in-
complete.
The framework should not output a global label such as “safe.” Its narrower result concerns whether
effective refusal has been demonstrated within the stated system, time, and scope.

4.1 External construct convergence and diagnostic refinement
The seven RET gates are best interpreted as a diagnostic refinement of broader control and oversight
constructs, not as seven uniquely natural categories. Several independent literatures converge on closely
related functional conditions.


                                                      8
Meaningful Human Control research emphasizes tracking of relevant human reasons and tracing out-
comes to responsible human agents (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018; Mecacci & Santoni de Sio,
2020). Recent work on meaningful medical-AI oversight proposes four interlocking conditions: epis-
temic capacity, cognitive space, decisional authority, and intervention effectiveness (van de Sande et al.,
2026). Those conditions map approximately onto RET as follows: decisional authority to 𝐺1 ; epistemic
capacity to 𝐺2 and part of 𝐺7 ; cognitive space to 𝐺4 and workflow-related aspects of 𝐺3 ; and interven-
tion effectiveness to 𝐺3 and 𝐺5 . The EU AI Act’s Article 14 similarly combines human competence and
understanding with authority to disregard, override, reverse, intervene in, or stop high-risk AI operation.
RET deliberately separates some of these broad conditions more finely. In particular, practical access,
temporal sufficiency, and causal efficacy are kept distinct because a control can exist, be reachable, and
still arrive too late or fail to change the consequential trajectory. Execution independence and epistemic
autonomy are separated because an advanced or agentic system can appear controllable under benign
conditions while retaining the ability to subvert the execution path or shape the evidence on which over-
sight depends. AI-control research provides direct methodological support for evaluating safety protocols
under intentional subversion (Greenblatt et al., 2024).
The relation to assurance-case work is also structural. RET treats effective refusal as a top-level scoped
claim supported by explicit subclaims and evidence obligations, rather than as a single indicator or score
(Montgomery, 2026). This crosswalk provides convergent construct support for the functional content
of RET. It does not establish that seven gates are the unique or mathematically minimal decomposition.
RET therefore does not presume that seven gates must remain fixed: if a smaller or differently structured
decomposition is shown to preserve the same scoped top-level claim while matching or improving false-
reassurance resistance, diagnostic resolution, and reproducibility, the framework should be compressed
or revised accordingly.

Diagnostic omission rationale. A useful way to interpret the decomposition is to ask what class of false
reassurance becomes easier if a gate is not examined explicitly. Omitting 𝐺1 admits nominal controls
held by actors whose refusal is not binding. Omitting 𝐺2 admits blind oversight in which the human lacks
the information needed to know when intervention is warranted. Omitting 𝐺3 admits rights or controls
that cannot be reached in the actual workflow. Omitting 𝐺4 admits interventions that work only after the
relevant deadline. Omitting 𝐺5 admits symbolic or ceremonial controls that do not alter the consequen-
tial outcome. Omitting 𝐺6 admits refusal paths that disappear when the constrained system withholds
cooperation or routes around enforcement. Omitting 𝐺7 admits judgments whose entire evidentiary basis
can be shaped by the system being evaluated.
These are illustrative diagnostic failure classes, not a proof of logical independence or ontological neces-
sity for each gate. Their role is to make distinct ways of falsely concluding “effective refusal” visible to
an assessor.

5. Time Constraint as the Operational Core
The defining feature of this framework is not human participation in the abstract. It is human interven-
tion before the consequential transition becomes irreversible.
This is where the framework differs in emphasis from generic descriptions of oversight.
A human may receive notice, understand the system, and possess nominal override authority, yet still lack
effective control if the relevant intervention cannot take effect before the declared irreversibility deadline.
The irreversibility deadline is not a universal constant. It is the latest point, for the declared outcome and
scope, at which refusal can still prevent, redirect, or materially alter the consequential transition under
review. It may be a measured value, a bounded range, or an evidence-based conservative estimate.

If one or both bounds are materially unknown, 𝐺4 should remain Partially Verified or Unknown.
This timing condition is especially important as AI-enabled systems become faster, more autonomous, and
more tightly coupled to downstream processes. Recent meaningful-oversight work likewise emphasizes
that oversight becomes procedural rather than protective when humans lack time, authority, or practical
intervention capacity.
RET does not claim that timing itself is a new concern. Its role is narrower: timing is a mandatory
proof obligation for the claim that refusal remains effective before irreversibility.

6. Independence, Redundancy, and Resilience
A central distinction is:


                                     redundancy ≠ independence

Multiple reviewers, control layers, or oversight channels do not establish resilience merely by being
numerous.
If the conditions that support Human NO remain concentrated in a single relevant common cause, multiple
nominal layers may fail together.
RET and resilience should be kept distinct.
𝐺6 Execution Independence asks whether the current refusal path can actually take effect without being
wholly dependent on the constrained system’s unilateral cooperation.
Resilience analysis asks whether the collection of conditions that currently make RET demonstrable is
excessively concentrated in one relevant common cause.
Thus:


                                    more layers ⇏ more resilience

Within the declared operating scope, execution review should include the consequential paths by which
a refusal could be rendered ineffective, including whether an apparently successful stop can be nullified
by automatic continuation, automatic restart, or a parallel path that produces the same declared outcome.
This requirement does not imply publication of exploitation procedures. It requires only enough defensive
evidence to support the assessment.
Independence is not absolute. No socio-technical architecture is independent of every physical, institu-
tional, computational, or informational dependency.



                                                    10
The narrower claim is:
      Effective Human standing should not, where avoidable, depend on a single relevant
      common cause whose loss automatically erases all meaningful refusal capacity.
Resilience is not an eighth RET gate. It is a separate post-RET question about the durability of the
conditions that support a current result.

7. Dynamic RET
RET is not a static certification.


                                       𝑅𝐸𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑡0 , Ω) = 𝑇 𝑅𝑈 𝐸

does not imply:


                                       𝑅𝐸𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑡1 , Ω) = 𝑇 𝑅𝑈 𝐸.

Nor does a result automatically transfer from one materially changed system or operating scope to another.
For RET, a material change is a change reasonably capable of altering:
    • the evidence supporting one or more gates;
    • a gate status;
    • intervention or irreversibility timing;
    • the declared operating scope;
    • a consequential execution dependency;
    • the evidentiary basis of human judgment.
Relevant changes can include system capability, autonomy, authority scope, infrastructure, human orga-
nization, information dependencies, or operating environment.
A material change reopens the affected gate or gates and their direct dependencies. Evidence for un-
changed gates may be reused only when its provenance and applicability remain valid.
The central sequence is therefore:



Human standing → 𝑅𝐸𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑡, Ω) → resilience of effective refusal → revalidation / re-authorization

The arrows represent an assessment and governance sequence, not logical implication.
Human standing does not imply a RET pass.
A RET pass does not imply permanent resilience.
Resilience does not eliminate the need for later revalidation.

8. Re-Authorization
Re-authorization is a temporal checkpoint.
Its purpose is to prevent a past authorization or a past RET result from being silently carried forward after
the conditions supporting it have materially changed.


                                                     11
The governing rule is:


                           material change ⇒ revalidation / re-authorization

Re-authorization is not ceremonial approval.
It cannot cure a failed RET by declaration alone.
If effective refusal is no longer demonstrated, relevant deficiencies must first be addressed and RET
revalidated before re-authorization can carry operational meaning.
RET does not decide whether a Human NO ought to be exercised in a particular dispute. It tests whether
refusal capacity exists and is operationally effective. The substantive legal, ethical, or policy rule gov-
erning when refusal should be exercised, honored, limited, or overridden is separate.
This structure is consistent with broader governance approaches that treat monitoring, recovery, change
management, contestability, and temporal renewal as continuing processes rather than one-time events.
For future generations, the framework makes only a limited claim. Present institutions cannot literally
obtain future consent. They can, however, avoid unnecessarily foreclosing the future capacity to contest
and re-authorize persistent authority structures. UNESCO’s 1997 Declaration on Responsibilities of the
Present Generations Towards Future Generations explicitly links responsibilities to future generations
with preserving future freedom of choice over political, economic, and social systems.

9. Human Standing Under Capability Asymmetry
The framework does not claim that human judgment is always epistemically superior.
Indeed, as AI capability rises, human error-correction value may decline in some domains.
The paper’s narrower normative distinction is:


                             epistemic superiority ≠ constitutive authority

Being better at prediction, planning, or optimization does not by itself establish unilateral authority to
erase the standing of affected humans.
This is a normative proposition, not a mathematical consequence of RET.
RET can test whether refusal capacity is operationally present. It does not prove constitutive Human
standing, the correctness of a human decision, the legitimacy of a particular institution, or the moral
superiority of human judgment.
This claim has important limits.
Human NO is not an absolute veto. Emergencies, incapacity, serious third-party harm, and competing
rights may justify override under separately specified legal or ethical standards.
At broader social or civilizational scale, the paper does not define a single actor who “speaks for human-
ity.” Humanity is plural, and the exact institutional representation of that plurality is outside the core
contribution.
The framework therefore remains a test of effective refusal capacity and a framework for discussing
standing, not a complete constitutional theory of AI governance.



                                                    12
10. Civilizational-Scale Governance Extension
At broader social or civilizational scale, the framework does not introduce a second operational test.
It retains only the following governance considerations:
    • broader and more irreversible effects increase concern about unilateral authorization;
    • Human standing may need to be represented plurally rather than through one actor;
    • sufficient information and time for refusal remain necessary;
    • intervention must be effective rather than symbolic;
    • prior refusal should not be rendered meaningless by automatic continuation after material change;
    • future re-authorization capacity should remain meaningful.
The exact institutional design, quorum, credential structure, or representative mechanism is outside this
paper’s core contribution.

11. Practical Use
RET can be used as a design-review, assurance, audit, procurement, deployment, and change-
management framework.
A practical assessment should identify:
    • the specific system or version 𝑆 ;
    • the evaluation time 𝑡;
    • the operating scope Ω;
    • the declared consequential outcome and evaluation horizon;
    • evidence for each of the seven gates;
    • gate-level evidence states;
    • the timing model and uncertainty bounds where relevant;
    • known limitations and unknowns;
    • conditions that would require revalidation.
A RET result should remain scoped.
A previous demonstration of effective refusal should not be treated as a permanent certificate after mate-
rial changes.
This makes RET compatible with lifecycle-oriented approaches such as the NIST AI RMF, which empha-
sizes post-deployment monitoring, override, incident response, recovery, decommissioning, and change
management.
RET can also complement regulatory human-oversight requirements. Article 14 of the EU AI Act es-
tablishes a regulatory expectation that human overseers of high-risk systems be able, where appropriate,
to disregard, override, reverse, intervene in, or stop AI-mediated operation. RET asks the additional
operational question: has it been demonstrated that this authority is usable in time and capable of
changing the consequential trajectory?

11.3 What the reference cases establish
These cases are illustrative.
They show only that:
    • RET can be executed under declared assumptions;
    • gate-level evidence states can be recorded;
    • timing failure can be made explicit;
    • a scoped global interpretation can be reproduced from the declared evidence.
They do not show that RET has been empirically validated, calibrated across domains, or shown to predict
real-world safety outcomes.
A direct falsifier for a particular RET assessment is a case classified as RET demonstrated in which,
under the declared system, time, scope, outcome, and evidence, the Human NO nevertheless cannot alter
the consequential outcome before the declared irreversibility deadline. Such a result would show that
at least one gate, evidence rule, or boundary declaration was mis-specified. A repeatable class of such
counterexamples would challenge the completeness of the framework itself.

12. Limitations
The framework does not imply:
    • unanimous global consent;
    • permanent minority veto;
    • world government;
    • human infallibility;
    • prohibition of autonomous AI action;


                                                   15
    • future-generation interests overriding all present rights;
    • shutdown as the only refusal action.
It also does not claim:
    • absolute invulnerability against superintelligence;
    • a theorem for containing arbitrarily capable systems;
    • permanent Human supremacy;
    • absolute independence from every common cause;
    • one-time RET certification as permanent validity;
    • a mathematical proof of normative Human standing;
    • empirical validation of RET from the illustrative reference cases;
    • calibrated false-positive or false-negative rates across domains.
RET remains sensitive to the quality and completeness of evidence.
A false positive can occur if an assessor concludes that refusal is demonstrated while a hidden dependency,
bypass, restart path, timing error, or mistaken causal assumption makes the refusal ineffective in practice.
A false negative can occur if effective refusal exists but the evidence needed to verify one or more gates
is unavailable. For this reason, Unknown is not treated as Failed, but it is also not treated as PASS.
Institutional capture is only partly addressed. RET can reveal some failures of authority, information,
independence, and epistemic autonomy, but it does not establish that the relevant human decision-maker
is virtuous, impartial, representative, or politically legitimate.
RET also does not yet have empirically established inter-rater reliability. The evidence obligations and
declared assumptions are intended to make disagreements inspectable, but different assessors may still
classify borderline evidence differently. Such disagreement should be recorded rather than concealed by
a forced PASS.
Likewise, a RET pass does not determine whether exercising Human NO is ethically or legally correct.
Emergencies, incapacity, public-safety externalities, conflicting human rights, latency constraints, refusal
abuse, or serious third-party harm may require a separate substantive rule.
Most importantly:
      The framework does not guarantee Human NO against an arbitrarily capable system.
      It tests whether effective refusal has been demonstrated and whether the conditions
      supporting it are structured so that their loss is not made easy, silent, single-point, and
      irreversible.
The seven-gate decomposition has not yet been established as the unique or minimal construct for assess-
ing demonstrated effective refusal. Some gates may share evidence, causal dependencies, or diagnostic
overlap. Future comparative work should test whether alternative decompositions with fewer or differ-
ently structured obligations preserve the same false-positive resistance, diagnostic resolution, and scoped
assurance value.
A priority validation agenda is therefore empirical as well as conceptual: inter-rater reliability should be
measured on shared evidence packs; retrospective and prospective cases should test false-positive and
false-negative behavior; compressed decompositions should be compared against RET-7 for diagnostic
loss; and domain-specific evidence thresholds should be calibrated where sufficient data exist. Until such
work is available, RET should not be assigned a quantitative error rate or be claimed as an empirically
minimal gate set.

13. Public-Safety Boundary
RET has a genuine dual-use dimension.
The same categories that help defenders examine whether refusal is real can, if operationalized too specif-
ically in public, reveal how superficial compliance might be engineered around an evaluation.
The public paper therefore publishes:
    • the seven defensive properties;
    • the timing and causal-efficacy principles;
    • the requirement to assess relevant common-cause dependence;
    • the dynamic nature of RET;
    • the need for revalidation and re-authorization;
    • evidence obligations and limitations.
It does not publish:
    • exploitation-ready bypass procedures;
    • exact failure-inducing sequences;
    • system-specific critical weak points;
    • detailed timing-manipulation procedures;
    • concrete methods for preserving formal approval while defeating causal efficacy;
    • attack-oriented dependency maps.
The publication rule is:


                           publish required properties and evidence obligations

while sequestering operational details that would materially increase evasion or bypass capability.
This manuscript does not incorporate non-public material from the separate fourth-paper line of research.

14. Discussion
The central distinction of this paper is simple:


                                 human presence ≠ effective Human NO

Existing MHC, meaningful-oversight, interruptibility, authority-allocation, reversibility, and assurance
research already provides substantial foundations for human control, intervention, contestability, and
lifecycle governance. The contribution of RET is not to relabel those concepts as new.
RET instead organizes them around a narrower top-level claim:
      For this system, at this time, and within this scope, has it been demonstrated that an
      independent Human NO can still change the consequential outcome before irreversibil-
      ity?
That framing produces four practical consequences.
First, time is not an implementation detail. It is a proof obligation for effective pre-irreversibility refusal.
Second, causal efficacy matters more than nominal authorization.
Third, redundancy must not be mistaken for execution independence or resilience.


                                                      17
Fourth, a result is evidence- and scope-dependent and may expire after material change.
The seven-gate structure is intentionally non-compensatory. Excellent evidence on authority or docu-
mentation cannot substitute for intervention that arrives too late, cannot change the outcome, or depends
entirely on the constrained system’s cooperation.
The reference cases also clarify what RET is and is not. It is executable as a structured evidence assess-
ment under declared assumptions. It is not yet an empirically calibrated predictor of safety, and it does
not convert normative Human standing into an engineering theorem.
The framework is intentionally modest about post-threshold guarantees. It does not claim that humans
can permanently constrain an arbitrarily capable system. Its aim is to identify and audit conditions under
which effective refusal is demonstrable before the relevant consequential transition becomes irreversible.

15. Conclusion
The operational core of the paper is:
      Within the remaining time before irreversibility, can an independent Human NO actu-
      ally change the outcome?
RET answers that question through seven non-compensatory proof obligations:


                       𝑅𝐸𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑡, Ω) = 𝐺1 ∧ 𝐺2 ∧ 𝐺3 ∧ 𝐺4 ∧ 𝐺5 ∧ 𝐺6 ∧ 𝐺7 .

RET is demonstrated only when all seven gates are Verified within the declared system, time, and oper-
ating scope.
A Failed gate means effective refusal is not demonstrated in that scope. Partial or Unknown evidence
does not establish failure, but it also cannot be promoted to PASS.
The result is neither a safety certificate nor a proof of control over superintelligence.
It is a scoped structured-evidence test of effective refusal.
The broader framework then asks whether the conditions supporting that result are resilient to rel-
evant common causes and whether material changes trigger revalidation and, where appropriate,
re-authorization.
The paper’s strongest defensible claim is therefore limited:
      The Refusal Option provides a structured way to determine whether pre-irreversibility
      effective Human refusal has been demonstrated through jointly verified authority,
      information, access, timing, causal efficacy, execution independence, and epistemic
      autonomy—and to identify where that claim fails or remains evidentially incomplete.
This claim does not replace MHC, oversight, assurance, contestability, or irreversibility research. It
supplies a narrower assessment target that those broader frameworks can support.
